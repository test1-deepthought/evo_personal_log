The meaning of life is not a single fact to be discovered but a question whose answer depends on which foundational assumptions you adopt. Across human history, seven major philosophical frameworks have emerged — theistic, Aristotelian, existentialist, utilitarian, nihilist, Stoic, and Buddhist — each giving a coherent answer grounded in distinct presuppositions about God, nature, self, value, and freedom. ## Derived Conclusions

Seven major frameworks were derived, each from a distinct cluster of assumptions:

| # | Framework | Core Answer | Key Assumptions |
|---|-----------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Theistic** | To know, love, and serve God; to attain eternal union with the divine. | A transcendent God exists; the universe has teleological order; sacred revelation provides truth. |
| 2 | **Aristotelian / Eudaimonic** | To flourish (*eudaimonia*) through rational activity and virtue cultivation in accordance with human nature. | Humans have a specific nature; rationality is the highest capacity; virtues are objective excellences. |
| 3 | **Existentialist** | Meaning is *created* by individuals through authentic choices in an absurd, purposeless universe. | Existence precedes essence; humans are radically free; the cosmos has no inherent purpose. |
| 4 | **Utilitarian** | To maximize overall well-being and reduce suffering for all sentient beings, impartially considered. | Well-being is intrinsically valuable; everyone counts equally; we ought to maximize welfare. |
| 5 | **Nihilist** | Life is meaningless — there is no objective purpose, value, or meaning; attempts to find any are self-deception. | No objective values exist; subjective meaning cannot replace objective meaning; the cosmos is purposeless. |
| 6 | **Stoic** | To live in accordance with nature/reason (Logos), cultivating virtue and inner tranquility while accepting what is beyond control with equanimity. | The universe is governed by rational natural law; inner virtue can be cultivated regardless of externals. |
| 7 | **Buddhist** | To attain liberation from suffering (*nirvana*) by overcoming attachment and craving, realizing impermanence and non-self, and cultivating compassion. | Suffering is inherent due to craving; liberation is attainable; there is no permanent self. |

**Synthesis:** There is no single universally agreed meaning; the question admits multiple valid answers depending on one's foundational presuppositions.

**Convergence:** Despite deep metaphysical disagreements, most frameworks converge on four practical goods:
1. **Reducing suffering** — directly in utilitarianism and Buddhism; indirectly in others
2. **Cultivating wisdom or understanding** — rational contemplation (Aristotelian), truth-seeking (theistic), insight into reality (Buddhist, Stoic)
3. **Relating meaningfully to others** — love (theistic), friendship (Aristotelian), compassion (Buddhist), impartial concern (utilitarian)
4. **Exercising agency with responsibility** — authentic choice (existentialist), virtue cultivation (Stoic, Aristotelian), moral duty (theistic, utilitarian)
