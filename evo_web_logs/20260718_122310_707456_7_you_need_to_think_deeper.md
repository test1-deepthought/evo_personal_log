**Layer 1 — The terrain of answers.** Each philosophical tradition offers a coherent answer, but they contradict each other. No single answer is universally valid; each is valid *only under its own assumptions*.

**Layer 2 — The logical structure.** The 11 answers fall into a spectrum: **Objective** (Theistic, Aristotelian — meaning exists independent of minds) → **Transcendent** (Buddhist, Theistic — meaning points beyond the finite) → **Intersubjective** (Humanist, Franklian — meaning is constructed by communities) → **Subjective** (Sartrean, Absurdist — meaning is created by individuals) → **Emergent** (Scientific — meaning arises from evolved brains) → **Denial** (Nihilist — meaning does not exist at all).

**Layer 3 — The meta-observation.** One conclusion — `asking_is_meaningful` — is unique: it depends on **zero assumptions**. It is universally robust. It states: the very act of asking the question reveals that humans are meaning-seeking creatures. A self-aware species interrogating its own existence is itself the most significant fact about that existence. The search may *be* the meaning.

**Layer 4 — The deepest synthesis.** Across all meaning-affirming perspectives (all except nihilism), four patterns recur:
1. **Transcendence of narrow self-interest** — every tradition directs attention beyond the ego
2. **Active engagement** — meaning requires doing, choosing, creating, not passive receipt
3. **Connection to something larger** — God, community, truth, wisdom, future generations, the cosmos
4. **Relationality** — meaning arises in relationship, not in isolation

The answer to "what is the meaning of life?" is therefore not a proposition but an *activity*: the activity of asking, seeking, choosing, creating, connecting, and transcending. The question does not have an answer; it IS the answer.
